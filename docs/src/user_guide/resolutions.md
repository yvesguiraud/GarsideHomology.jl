# Germs and resolutions

```@meta
CurrentModule = GarsideHomology
```

This page gives details on the construction of the germ and of the reduced lcm resolution, taking the example of the classical braid monoid ``B⁺(A₂)``.

```julia
using GarsideHomology, Chevie

W = coxgroup(:A, 2)
G = germ(W)             # simples e, 1, 2, 12, 21, 121
P = lcm_resolution(G)
```

The resulting resolution has one generating cell in degree ``0``, two in degree ``1``, and one in degree ``2``. By default, in the REPL, the resolution is displayed by its list of generating cells and boundaries in a possibly abridged form:

```julia-repl
julia> P
LCMResolution: 4 cells, by degree [1, 2, 1], …
Cells:
  0: e
  1: 1, 2
  2: 2|12
Differential:
  (boundaries of degree 1 omitted, such as d(1) = 1[] - []1)
  d(2|12) = …
```

The complete resolution can be written using `show(stdout, "text/plain", P)`.

## The germ

The germ `G` stores the simples of the Garside monoid and operations on them.

The simples are stored as [`GermElement`](@ref)s, and the complete list is given by `simples(G)`. The simples can be constructed by applying the germ to a list of atoms or to a string corresponding to the display of the simple:
- `G(1, 2)` is the product of the atoms ``1`` and ``2``;
- `G("12")` is the same simple read from its written form;
- `G()` is the identity.

The operations stored by the germ are:
- the product `s * t`, defined when the result is a simple:
  - `G(1) * G(2) == G("12")`,
  - `G(1) * G(1)` throws an `ArgumentError`;
- the right complement `s \ t`, defined when `s ≼ t`.

If `s` is a simple, then it can be written as a product of atoms, and it admits in general several such writings: `word(s)` returns the smallest of those representative words, for the deglex order generated by the order in which the atoms are given. The length of `word(s)` is given by `length(s)`.

## Construction of the lcm resolution

The generating cells are built as a tree, starting from the generating cell `e` of degree ``0``. Each generating cell ``x = x₁|⋯|xₙ``, of weight ``ω = x₁⋯xₙ``, has a list of *admissible atoms*: all the atoms, in a fixed order, for `e`, and otherwise the atoms that gave the elder siblings of ``x`` (the children of the same parent built before ``x``), in their order. The children of ``x`` are then built as follows:
- the admissible atoms of ``x`` are sorted by a preorder on the right lcms ``ω ∨ a``, ties keeping the order of the list;
- an atom ``a`` is kept if no atom placed before ``a`` in the sorted list divides ``ω ∨ a``;
- each atom ``a`` that is kept gives a child of ``x``, the generating cell ``x₁|⋯|xₙ|ω \ (ω ∨ a)`` of weight ``ω ∨ a``, whose admissible atoms are the atoms kept before ``a``.

In the package, the preorder and the order of the atoms at the root are given by an [`LCMOrdering`](@ref):

```julia
P = lcm_resolution(G)                                   # the reduced lcm resolution
D = lcm_resolution(G, LCMOrdering(TrivialPreorder()))   # the Dehornoy–Lafont resolution
```

The possible preorders are:
- [`ComplementDeglex`](@ref), the default, orders the atoms ``a`` at a simple ``ω`` by the deglex order of the right complements of ``ω`` in ``(ω ∨ a)``. Two atoms are tied only if they have the same lcm with ``ω``. This preorder gives the reduced lcm resolution.
- [`TrivialPreorder`](@ref) ties all the lcms, so that the construction keeps the order of the root everywhere. This preorder gives the Dehornoy–Lafont resolution.
- [`FunctionPreorder`](@ref) takes a function `lt(G, ω, x, y)`, which says whether the lcm `x` precedes the lcm `y` at the simple `ω`. A preorder that is not compatible with divisibility may give a resolution that is not reduced, so that the result should be checked by its certificate; [`extends_divisibility`](@ref) tests the compatibility with the divisibility.

## Generating cells of the lcm resolution

The functions `ncells(P)` and `ncells(P, n)` give the total number of generating cells of `P` and the number of generating cells of degree `n`, respectively. The generating cells of degree `n` form a basis of the module `P[n]`, accessible through `basis(P[n])`: on our example, `basis(P[1])` contains the generating cells `1` and `2`. The function `basis(P)` returns all the generating cells as a graded object, given degree by degree.

The generating cells can be constructed using e.g. `Cell(P, "2|12")` or `Cell(P, G(2), G(1, 2))`; the unique generating cell of degree ``0`` is `Cell(P)`. A [`Cell`](@ref) `x` is treated as a sequence of letters, with the accessors and functions like `x[1]`, `x[end]`, or `length(x)`. The following functions are defined:
- the weight `cell_weight(x)`, that is, the product `x₁…xₙ` for `x = x₁|⋯|xₙ`;
- the homological degree `cell_degree(x)`;
- the sequence of atoms `atom_sequence(x)` corresponding to the generating cell (in particular, the weight is the lcm of those atoms).

## Elements of the free modules

The elements of the free modules composing the resolution are sums of terms `c×σ[x]τ`: a generating cell `x`, multiplied by simples `σ` and `τ` on both sides and by an integer `c`. The corresponding type is [`BasedFreeModuleElement`](@ref).

The elements can be built from generating cells, read from their written form, and combined, as in the following examples:

```julia
a = P("1")                      # the generator [1]
P("2×1[2]1 - [1]")              # written -[1] + 2×1[2]1
G(1) * P("2") == P("1[2]")      # the action on the left
a * G(2) == P("[1]2")           # and on the right
```

The identity is omitted on both sides, and so are the coefficients `±1`; the generating cell of degree ``0``, when seen as an element of the free module, is written `[]`. The marker `×` separates a coefficient from what follows when the atoms are written with digits. If `a` is an element of the free module, then `a[σ, x, τ]` is the coefficient of the term `σ[x]τ` in `a`, and one can iterate over `a` as in `for (c, σ, x, τ) in a`.

## The differential of the lcm resolution

The differential of the resolution is of type [`Differential`](@ref) and it can be accessed as follows:

```julia
d = differential(P)
d(P("1"))                        # 1[] - []1
d(G(2) * y) == P("21[] - 2[]1")  # true
x = Cell(P, "2|12")
iszero(d(d(x)))                  # true
```

The call `d(x)` gives the boundary of the generator `[x]` corresponding to a generating cell `x`, and `d(a)` is that of an element `a` of the free module.

The matrix of the differential going from degree `n + 1` to degree `n` is given by `differential(P, n)`. It is of type [`LabelledSparseMatrix`](@ref), a matrix whose entries are labelled by the pairs `(σ, τ)` of simples acting on the left and on the right.

## Certificates on the lcm resolution

The function [`certify`](@ref)`(P)` checks the resolution and returns a [`ResolutionCertificate`](@ref), shown as a short report:
- correctness: homogeneity of the boundaries, ``d² = 0``, and Euler characteristic of the generating cells of each weight against the Möbius function ``μ(1, ω)``;
- minimality, that is, the absence of terms `1[x]1` in the differential;
- linearity, that is, every term `σ[x]τ` satisfies `length(σ) + length(τ) = 1`.

The check of ``d² = 0`` is the costliest: `certify(P; square_zero = false)` skips it. The keyword `nworkers` sets the number of tasks to be used. The Möbius function is computed by the crosscut theorem of Rota up to 16 atoms, and, above, by a pass over the intervals (keyword `mobius`).

The functions [`is_correct`](@ref), [`is_minimal`](@ref) and [`is_linear`](@ref) read the report. A failed check is reported, never thrown.
